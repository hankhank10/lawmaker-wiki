# Voter Demands & Pledges

Every so often the voters of a country make a **demand**: a specific, numeric fiscal ask. Answering it is free and popular. Delivering it is neither. This page is about that gap.

## What voters demand

A country has at most one open demand at a time, and it only appears where the country is a genuine outlier against its [peer countries](economy.md#outliers-are-where-the-votes-are). The target is always a real, challenging step rather than a freebie. Occasionally the voters skip the comparison and demand something arbitrary, such as a tax band slashed to next to nothing. These behave exactly like any other demand.

| Demand | What voters are asking for |
| --- | --- |
| **Tax cut** | Bring one income band's tax rate down to a stated figure. |
| **Spending rise** | Lift spending in a starved category to a stated per-person amount. |
| **End IMF control** | Get the country out of its [IMF programme](economy.md#imf-intervention). |

The figures are fixed when the demand is issued and never change afterwards, even if peer averages drift. Demands are one-sided on purpose: nobody marches for a balanced budget. Each demand is individually popular, and together they are fiscally reckless.

While a country is under an IMF programme, only the end-IMF demand can appear, because the emergency austerity budget forbids delivering the others. It is the one demand that is responsible to keep: delivering it means holding the line on austerity until the programme ends.

## How a demand plays out

1. **Open.** Any party can pledge. Pledging closes at the next election, the *accountability election*.
2. **In delivery.** Every pledger is now on the hook until the *judgment election*, which is the first election held **at least two months after** the accountability election. An election inside that window (say, a snap election) is skipped for judgment. That is a deferral, not a pardon, but it guarantees you time to pass a delivering budget.
3. **Judged.** At the judgment election each pledger faces the reckoning below. If the demand is delivered at any point before then, it is marked fulfilled and nobody is judged.

"The next election" means the next one that actually happens, so a [snap election](elections.md#early-elections) pulls the whole timeline forward.

## Pledging

Pledging is **free**: no political power, no vote, no risk today. You do have to publish a short delivery plan, but it is pure flavour and has no effect on the boost, on judgment or on anything else.

The moment you pledge, your party gets the **People's Pledge**: a **×1.05** multiplier on your vote preference, lasting until the next election and including that election. The boost does not stack. Pledging two open demands leaves you with one boost, but each pledge is a separate liability at judgment. The modifier table is in [Campaigning](campaigning.md).

## The hangover

At the judgment election, each pledger is checked on two questions: do you hold power, and was the demand delivered?

- **Not in power:** you are off the hook. The boost was banked long ago.
- **In power and delivered:** safe. You kept your promise.
- **In power and not delivered:** you get **Broken Promises**, a **×0.85** multiplier on your vote preference for **24 months**.

The brand is applied **before the judgment election simulates**, so it hurts you in the very election where you are judged and again at the next one. The boost is small and short. The brand is much bigger, lasts far longer, and lands on an election.

### The government answers too

Not pledging is not an escape if you are in the cabinet. When a demand is judged unmet, **every cabinet party that did not pledge** gets **Ignored the Voters**: a **×0.93** multiplier for **12 months**. It is smaller than Broken Promises, so pledging is still the bigger bet, but a government can no longer stay clean by staying silent. Opposition parties are untouched. A party that pledged and is in the cabinet gets Broken Promises instead, never both for the same demand.

Pressure also builds before the election. While any in-delivery demand is still undelivered, every cabinet party carries **Restless Voters**, a mild **×0.98** that lapses shortly after the demand is delivered or judged. It does not stack.

### Penalties compound

Both penalties stack per demand. Each additional demand a party is branded for multiplies its penalty again, down to a floor of **×0.50**. Two broken promises cost ×0.85² (about ×0.72), and three ignored demands ×0.93³ (about ×0.80). Ignoring several demands is far worse than ignoring one. The stack ends when the brand expires, and every new breach pushes that expiry out.

### The power test, in detail

You hold power at judgment if **any** of these is true at that moment:

| Test | You hold power if... |
| --- | --- |
| **Cabinet** | your party holds a current cabinet seat (or the head-of-government office) |
| **Elected office** | your party holds any elected office, such as a presidency |
| **Top-3 with presence** | in any multi-seat legislature you are in the top 3 by seats **and** hold at least 10% of its seats |

The 10% floor stops a tiny third party from being branded for a budget it could never move. Power is measured on judgment day, not before.

## Delivery is collective

A tax cut or spending rise is delivered by a **passed budget** that reaches the target: the band's rate falls to (or below) the figure, or spending in the category rises to (or above) it. The end-IMF demand is delivered when the programme actually ends, once debt recovers ([how the programme ends](economy.md#how-the-programme-ends)).

Delivery belongs to the country, not to whoever proposed the budget. If a passing budget hits the target, the demand is delivered for everyone who pledged. Delivery is also sticky: once delivered, always delivered, even if a later budget reverses the change. But budgets need a majority, so you can promise alone yet only deliver together.

!!! warning "The winner's trap"
    The party most likely to win is the party most tempted to pledge, and the one most exposed. Win power without the votes to pass the budget, and your popularity boost turns into a Broken Promises brand at the worst moment. Only pledge what your likely coalition can actually pass. If you are in the cabinet you are exposed either way, so the real question is whether to take the boost on the way in.

!!! tip "The opposition's free lunch"
    If you expect to be in opposition on judgment day, pledging is close to free: you take the boost and you are off the hook. The exception is **end IMF control**, which you keep by being disciplined rather than generous. A cabinet party has no such lunch: delivering the demand, or paying for it, is the only way out.
