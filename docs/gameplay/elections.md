# Elections & Voters

Elections turn your voting record into seats. Understanding how electors think is the single most important skill in Lawmaker, because everything you do is ultimately judged by them.

## How elections work

Elections run on a **regular schedule** set per legislature, and every party takes part automatically. On election day the electors vote and the count takes place live over the day. Once it is complete, seats are shared out **proportionally** to each party's vote share (10% of the votes gives roughly 10% of the seats), and the new legislature and [government formation](cabinet.md) take effect overnight.

A country's [elected offices](#elected-offices), such as a presidency, are counted in the same election on their own schedules, but each goes to a single candidate rather than being split into seats.

## The electoral system

Seats are allocated with the **D'Hondt method**, as in Spain, Portugal and Poland. It guarantees that a party with over half the vote gets at least half the seats, so a decisive winner is never locked out by rounding. Some legislatures also set a **minimum threshold** (often around 5%) below which a party wins no seats, so check your country's rules.

## Understanding electors

Your country's population is modelled as individual **electors**, each with a job, income band, education, housing and religious background, a political worldview and personal life goals. Electors really do read your record and respond to it.

### Retirement

Electors retire by **age**, not by chance. Once an elector reaches the country's retirement age (the **Pension Age** law) they are shown as retired, but keep their working history on record. Changing the law takes effect immediately. The retired share of the population moves the labour force and GDP, so pension policy is an economic lever as well as a social one; see the [Economy](economy.md).

### Suffrage

A country's constitution can restrict who may vote, by gender, age, employment, income, education or housing. The rules and their costs are in [Suffrage](constitution.md#suffrage). What it means for elections:

- **Disenfranchised electors are left out of polls and counts**, but turnout still divides by the *whole* population, so a restricted franchise shows up as lost turnout.
- If the rules leave **fewer than 25** analysed electors eligible, the electorate is too small to stand for the country. No election is held that round, every seat stays with its holder, and polls report "too few eligible voters" instead of a result.

### How an elector decides

1. They review how each party **voted** on proposals.
2. They compare those votes to **their own** positions and priorities.
3. **Recent votes count more** than old ones, so your record is always live.
4. They form an opinion of each party and vote for the best match.

!!! example
    A committed environmentalist sees the Green Alliance voted for renewables and against coal subsidies, while the Business Party did the opposite. They vote Green.

Each vote's weight fades steadily with age. Last week's vote counts almost in full, and one from more than about 500 game days ago counts for nothing.

!!! note "Budgets count too, about 30%"
    Voters judge you on more than laws. By default an elector's view is roughly **70% your law record** and **30% your budgets**: they react to tax changes in their own income band and to spending shifts in areas they care about. A party that ignores budgets hands rivals a free advantage. See [How budgets affect voters](economy.md#how-budgets-affect-voters).

!!! note "When nobody impresses an elector"
    Votes are never wasted: an elector with no positive opinion still splits their vote among the parties they are neutral about or dislike least. A party with **no recent voting record** is unknown rather than neutral and gets none of these votes, so a brand-new party can't sweep the disaffected.

## National moods

A country can carry **national moods**: trends that nudge how voters weigh issues, by shifting positions, changing how *important* an issue feels, or both. When a mood lines up with your platform it is a tailwind, so propose into it. When it is against you, focus elsewhere and wait, since temporary moods pass.

Some moods are **permanent**, set when a country is created:

| Mood | Tilt |
| --- | --- |
| Traditional Culture | Traditional gender roles and religion; anti-immigration |
| Pioneer Spirit | Liberty and free markets; anti-welfare |
| Common Ground | Welfare state and worker rights |
| Stewards of the Land | Environment over military |
| Strength through Strength | Military and law-and-order |
| Citizens of the World | Gender equality and immigration |
| Patriarchal Society | Traditional gender roles; gender issues weigh more |
| Deeply Polarised | No shift; every issue matters more |
| The Great Disillusionment | No shift; every issue matters less, elections get noisier |
| Nation Builders | Infrastructure and education spending |
| Silver Republic | Pro-older voters; elder issues weigh more |
| Secular Republic | Secular governance and liberty |
| Bread and Circuses | Wages, welfare and prices weigh more; culture wars fade |

Others are **event-triggered**: a **Constitutional Crisis** (a monarch vetoes a passed bill; see [Hereditary Monarchy](monarchy.md#constitutional-crisis)), a **Constitutional Convention** (relaxes the rules for constitutional change for a year; see [Constitutional conventions](constitution.md#constitutional-conventions)), a **Suffrage Crisis** (below), or an **Economic Miracle / Crash** that bends the growth rate (see [Economy](economy.md#national-moods-and-economic-growth)).

### Suffrage Crisis

A **Suffrage Crisis** starts automatically when a constitutional [Suffrage](constitution.md#suffrage) change strips the vote from at least **10%** of the electors who could vote before it. It lasts **180 days**, and restricting the franchise again while it is active resets the clock. It doesn't sway any elector; what it changes is the constitutional rulebook: a package made up only of Suffrage changes that are each unchanged or less restrictive can be opened during the normal cooldown and votes at **half** the usual threshold. It won't expire while the electorate is too small to hold an election, so a country locked out of voting by its own rules always keeps an easier way back.

## Elector satisfaction

Each elector also has a **satisfaction** score: how happy they are with the *state of the country*, separate from their opinion of any party. It compares your finances with similar **peer countries**, mostly the tax rate on their income band (about 70%) and spending on the issues they care about (about 30%). High satisfaction favours incumbents; low satisfaction opens the door for opposition parties running on change. It doesn't change votes directly and only refreshes when a poll or election happens.

## Turnout

Not every elector votes every time. Controversial recent proposals and close races push turnout up; long inactivity pushes it down. A custom [logo](parties.md#party-branding) gives a +5% turnout boost among your supporters, and going without costs you 5%.

## Polling

A party can **commission a poll** to see projected vote shares, turnout and seat estimates. It costs **party money**, not PP, and a bigger sample is more accurate:

| Poll | Sample | Accuracy | Cost |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Quick | 40% | Low | $5,000 |
| Extensive | 65% | Moderate | $15,000 |
| Comprehensive | 95% | High | $30,000 |

Party funds come from fundraising campaign events; see [Campaigning](campaigning.md). A poll is a snapshot, not a guarantee, since voters keep reacting to new votes right up to election day.

A commissioned poll is **private to your party** until you choose to **publish** it, which makes it visible to every party in the country. Publish it when you want to build trust with coalition partners or set expectations; keep it private for secret strategy. There are also **public polls** that any logged-in user can view.

## Early elections

A party can call an election before the scheduled date for **30 PP**, or **10 PP** if the chamber voting on the call holds no seats. The call is put to a vote lasting **60 days**, or **5 days** when no party in the voting chamber holds seats. It goes ahead unless **50% or more** oppose it. Leading parties use this to lock in gains; opposition parties vote it down to deny them the moment. The election is held 10 game days after the call passes.

The vote **concludes early** once the outcome is certain: when yes has secured more than 50% of occupied seats, or when yes can no longer reach 50% however the remaining votes fall.

### Automatic early elections

The game calls an early election with no vote and no PP cost, held 10 game days later, in two cases:

- **A chamber empties below 50% occupancy**, usually after a governing party is disbanded.
- **An elected office is vacant**, has held an election before, and at least one party has a valid candidate standing for it. An office that has never held an election waits for its first scheduled one.

An [upheld impeachment](supreme-court.md) schedules an election directly.

## Elected offices

An **elected office** is a single post, such as a presidency, held by one person. Countries name their own offices and decide how often each is elected. The [constitution](constitution.md) says whether an office has a [veto](legislation.md#elected-office-veto), whether it heads the government, and which powers it holds.

### Candidacies

Each party can put up **one candidate per office**, chosen from its own activists, and declaring or changing a candidate costs **20 PP**.

- A character can stand for **one office at a time**, and can't stand for a second office while holding one.
- The candidate must be a non-retired, non-expelled activist of your party who hasn't been [removed from that office by the Supreme Court](supreme-court.md). An [arrested](executive-actions.md#order-arrest) activist can be declared, but their candidacy is ignored at the count while they are under arrest.
- You can change or withdraw your candidate at any time **except while a count that includes the office is running**. A candidacy stays on file after the election, so a sitting holder stands again by default.

**How it is won.** The candidate with the most votes wins and a tie is settled at random. Votes for a party with no valid candidate aren't wasted: they flow to the candidates in proportion to their parties' vote shares. If nobody has a valid candidate, the office is left **vacant**. The winner takes office when the election is processed, and changing a party's candidate afterwards never changes who holds it.

**Vacancies.** An office falls vacant at once, with no successor, when the holder is removed by an upheld impeachment (and barred from that office for good), is expelled, retires, or loses their party to disbandment, or otherwise stops qualifying. While it is vacant its powers are unavailable, it has no veto, it is nobody's head of government, and a cabinet it appoints can't be re-formed (the sitting cabinet stays). Only an election fills it, and once the office has held an election the game refills it automatically: with at least one valid candidate standing, an [automatic early election](#automatic-early-elections) follows 10 game days later. If you want the seat filled sooner, put a candidate up.

### No-confidence and vacant-office calls

A single occupant can't send their own office to the country, so a call on an office is decided by the constitution's **no-confidence chamber**.

| Office | Call | Cost | Needed to pass |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Held | Vote of no confidence in the holder | 30 PP | The constitution's threshold, typically 75% of votes cast |
| Vacant | Call to refill the office | 10 PP | Simple majority |

Abstentions are ignored. If the call passes, the election is held 10 game days after voting ends, and a holder serves until then.
