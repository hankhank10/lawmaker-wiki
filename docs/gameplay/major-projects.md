# Major Projects

Before an election, an established party can promise a huge, multi-year capital project: new hospitals, a nuclear plant, a high-speed rail line. If it wins that election, the project is built automatically and the *country* pays for it. Committing costs the party only [political power](../reference.md#action-costs). It is the easiest popular promise in the game, and the most expensive one to the treasury.

This is different from a [pledge](demands.md). A pledge is free but leaves you liable if the demand isn't delivered. A Major Project commitment costs PP up front, never comes back to haunt you with a penalty, and delivers itself the moment you win.

## Who can commit

Your party can commit if **all** of these hold:

- You are the party's **owner (leader)**, and the party is active (not disbanded, not enthroned).
- The party is **established**: old enough (the [minimum age](../reference.md#key-timers) is in the Quick Reference), **or** it currently holds power (a cabinet seat, an elected office, or a top-three place holding at least 10% of a chamber's seats, the same [power test](demands.md#the-power-test-in-detail) used for pledges). A freshly founded party, including one re-founded after disbanding, has to wait or win power first. Reviving an old party keeps its age.
- The country has a running economy and is **not under an [IMF programme](economy.md#imf-intervention)**.
- You have **no commitment still pending**. Projects already being built don't count, so a party can have several delivering at once.
- The **cooldown** since your party's last commitment has passed, whatever happened to that commitment.
- The commit window is open (see below) and you can pay the [PP cost](../reference.md#action-costs).

The PP is **never refunded**, whether the commitment lapses, the sponsor disbands or the IMF cancels it. There is no withdrawing or editing a commitment once made. The [cost](../reference.md#action-costs), cooldown, minimum age and window lengths are all in the [Quick Reference](../reference.md#key-timers).

## Which election

You commit ahead of "the next election": the soonest election in the country, across every legislature and elected office. A run-off doesn't count, because it is the second round of a contest already under way.

- Commitments open well ahead of that election and close shortly before it (exact lengths in the [Quick Reference](../reference.md#key-timers)). After that they are closed country-wide until the election has been held.
- If several bodies vote on that date (say the presidency and a chamber), you choose **one** as your **binding election**. You cannot skip ahead to a later election, and if a different body's election is scheduled earlier later on, your target doesn't change.
- If the elections are staggered closely, the window for the second can already be shut when the first has passed. A presidency on 1 March and a chamber on 5 March means nobody can commit to the chamber at all.
- An [early election](elections.md#early-elections) can pull your target's date forward. The commitment simply resolves at the new, earlier election.
- To commit to an **elected office** you must be fielding **your own valid candidate** for it. Endorsing another party's candidate does not count, and if your candidate stops being valid before the result is decided, the commitment lapses.

## The boost

From the moment you commit, your party gets a vote-share boost among electors who **want more spending on that project's budget line**. Electors who oppose the spending are simply unmoved: nobody is put off. Keen, high-salience electors are boosted most, so a project is worth most where plenty of voters care about its line. Today rail, roads, energy and airports all appeal to the same electors, because they share one infrastructure issue.

- It shows in **polls** immediately.
- It stacks multiplicatively with your other [modifiers](campaigning.md), such as the People's Pledge. A party has at most one pending commitment, so it never stacks with itself.
- It applies to **every election held on the target's day**, legislative and single-seat alike, but only your binding election decides whether the project happens. On an office ballot it only works if your party is on that ballot with its own candidate.
- It **disappears the moment the binding election is decided**, win or lose. It is an election-eve boost, not a lasting one.

## Winning

- **Office:** your party holds the office once the result has been processed. If the election goes to a [run-off](elections.md#run-offs), you stay pending only if you are a finalist, and the second round decides. Backing the winner doesn't count, and neither does a winner whose candidacy turned invalid.
- **Legislature:** your party holds **strictly the most seats**. A tie for first is not a win.
- If the election can't be held (too few eligible voters), the commitment lapses.

If you don't win, the commitment lapses with no cost beyond the PP already spent and no penalty. The cooldown still runs from the day you committed. A pending commitment whose election never resolves is also lapsed automatically about a month after the election was due.

## What happens if you win

The project becomes its own **monthly budget line**, starting on the 1st of the next month and running for the project's duration. The total is fixed when you commit, as a share of that day's GDP, and the treasury pays it in equal monthly instalments.

- **No vote is needed and nobody can cancel it.** No government, legislature or rival can stop a delivering project. It still counts as capital investment in the matching [infrastructure](economy.md#infrastructure) asset at 100%, so it genuinely builds something.
- It is a **separate line**: it doesn't count towards voter demands, budget opinion or satisfaction. What it does is widen the deficit, so everyone's later budgets have to work around it.
- Two winning parties with the same project mean you pay for both.
- When the window ends, the project completes.

At catalogue sizes one project won't bring in the IMF by itself. Several stacked on top of an already indebted country can.

## The only two ways out

1. **The sponsor disbands** (including an inactivity auto-disband). A pending commitment lapses. A delivering project stops from the next month, or is removed if it hasn't started. Nothing is refunded and reviving the party restarts nothing. That makes [disbanding](parties.md) a drastic exit for a sponsor who regrets winning.
2. **The country enters an [IMF programme](economy.md#imf-intervention).** Every commitment in the country, pending and delivering, is cancelled, with no extra blame on anyone. Nothing is restored when the programme ends. Commitments reopen under the normal rules.

An enthroned sponsor loses only its *pending* commitments; delivering projects continue.

## Should you commit?

!!! tip "Cheap for you, dear for everyone else"
    If you are likely to win the binding election, the cost to you is the PP and the cooldown. The country carries the rest, and a winner who inherits a deficit pays for it in the next [budgets](economy.md#how-budgets-affect-voters). If you are likely to lose, the commitment is a PP bet that the boost tips a close race.

!!! warning "You can't take it back"
    Win and the project is built whether or not you still want it. Check the country's debt first: a deep hole plus a big project is how you hand the country to the [IMF](economy.md#imf-intervention), which cancels the project and brands the parties blamed.
